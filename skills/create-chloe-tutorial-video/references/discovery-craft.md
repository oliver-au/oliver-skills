# Discovery craft: question-driven explainers for a 10–12 year old

Chloe’s lessons start with something the learner can inspect. A prediction gives her a reason to watch what happens. A fresh problem lets her try the same reasoning herself.

## Choose the opening

When two answers are easy to confuse, put them to a visible test. Let Chloe make her own prediction before signalling the solution. A character can voice a plausible guess in the worked example. Keep independent practice free of hints until after the thinking pause.

For a new procedure, work one example and let Chloe finish the next. For a passage or a claim, let her read the evidence and compare interpretations. Choose the route the knowledge point needs.

## The beats

Use these as a planning guide. Combine or omit beats to fit the idea. Protect the time to read, predict and check the answer.

| Beat | Length | Purpose | Must have |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Cold open | ≤ 10 s | A concrete object and a question that feels obvious. | The object is on screen in the first second. No title card, agenda or "today we'll learn". |
| 2. Commit | 6–10 s + 4 s quiet | She picks an answer. | Every given stays visible. Options look neutral. The tempting answer is said out loud with its reason ("They're the furthest apart, so surely…"). |
| 3. Test it | 10–20 s | The object answers, not the narrator. | A real transformation: fold, regroup, test, connect. The reveal is a visual event; narration reacts to it. |
| 4. Why it fooled us | 10–15 s | Name the trap and show its mechanism. | Same object, same colours. A memorable trap name. |
| 5. But wait (optional) | 8–15 s | A second surprise that deepens the idea. | Only when it adds understanding: an edge case, a shortcut that fails, the rule working somewhere unexpected. |
| 6. Exam bridge | As the question requires | Apply the idea in the selected section. | Match the official response format. For Writing, set an open task with purpose and audience. For multiple choice, check the alternatives against the givens. |
| 7. Your turn | 10–15 s + pause | Transfer: new surface, same deep structure. | Different numbers, story or shape. Reveal through the visual, not a text answer. |
| 8. Pocket rule | ≤ 8 s | One line to keep. | A short, accurate line with its necessary conditions. |

A typical lesson is 2–3 minutes. Do not pad: if the idea is small, merge 4 and 5, or skip 5.

## Show, don't tell

- Keep relevant evidence visible while the learner uses it. Hold still for reading, predictions and reflection.
- On-screen text is for labels, numbers and the single question being asked. Leave passages, rule conditions and options visible long enough to read.
- Synchronise decisive claims to the relevant visible evidence. For example, when the voice says "B and E touch", the shared edge lights up.
- When the narration says "look", "watch" or "see", something must visibly happen within a second.
- The image must prove the exact claim. If the narration says A, B and C meet at a corner, show the corner where A, B and C meet — not a different corner of the same cube.
- Prefer exact, model-driven visuals (a real 3D fold, real counts, real coordinates) over approximate drawings.

## Tension tools

- **Say the wrong answer kindly.** "It feels obvious that…", "I'd have bet on E". Treat the mistake as reasonable; never mock it.
- **Open loop.** Pose the question early and keep it unresolved until the visual answers it.
- **Contrast.** Place the intuitive picture and the true picture side by side, using the same object.
- **Slow down at the reveal.** A half-second of silence, then the event, then the words.
- **No invented facts.** Do not claim "most students get this wrong" or "this appears in every test" without a source. "It feels obvious" or a named character's guess does the same job honestly.

## Voice for a Year 5 learner

- Speak to one child. Use "you", "we", "let's" and the occasional "I". Warm, curious, a little playful.
- Average sentence ≤ 15 words. One idea per sentence.
- Year 5 vocabulary. Replace jargon or define it once with a picture:

  | Avoid | Say |
  | --- | --- |
  | derive | work out |
  | mutually adjacent | all touch each other |
  | explicit | shown / written down |
  | hypothesis | guess / idea to test |
  | inference | a clue-based guess (define once) |
  | counterexample | one example that breaks the rule (define once) |

- Australian English by default: maths, centre, colour, neighbour, practise (verb).
- Give the trap a name she can remember: *the percentage trap*, *the far-apart trap*, *the story you added*.
- The pocket rule is short enough to say in one breath: "Three straight squares: the ends are opposite."

## Exam realism

- Check the current official NSW Selective format before writing the exam bridge. Use the official practice material for the requested subject. Treat average time per item as pacing guidance.
- Match the answer-option count and layout of current official practice material. Keep options parallel in length, grammar and confidence words: if only the correct option says "probably", the test-wise child learns the wrong lesson.
- Every distractor represents a real mistake. Say in one line why each is wrong.
- Model what to do when stuck: eliminate what is impossible, then choose among what is left.
- Label original questions as original teaching examples, not official items.

## In a series

- Reuse trap names across lessons so they become vocabulary.
- End with, or start the practice with, one quick question from an earlier lesson (spaced, mixed retrieval).
- Keep a recognisable voice and visual hierarchy across the series. Choose a cast, setting or diagram that fits each problem. Carry the same object through a shot when its continuity is part of the reasoning.

## Script before code

Write the full narration and a beat sheet before building scenes. Then run the script critique in [review checks](review-checks.md#script-critique-before-building). Fixing a weak hook in text costs minutes; fixing it after rendering costs hours.
